**Canopy cover** measures the percentage of land covered by the tops of trees, as seen from above. It tells us how much of the landscape is shaded by tree foliage --- which helps scientists assess forest density, structure, and the capacity of an ecosystem to support wildlife, regulate climate, and recover from disturbance. TCC is expressed as a percentage from 0% to 100%. It's a powerful way to track how ecosystems respond to invasive species, deforestation, restoration, or natural regrowth.

-   0 to 10%: Bare or deforested. Little to no tree cover. Can indicate heavily degraded landscapes, shrubland, grassland, or areas recently cleared by humans, herbivores, fire, or storms.

-   10 to 25%: Sparse canopy (early recovery or open ecosystems). Scattered tree cover or low-stature vegetation. May reflect recovering forests after disturbance, or naturally sparse systems like savannas. Vulnerable to erosion and invasive species without further recovery if previously forested.

-   25 to 75%: Moderate canopy (recovering or mixed). Partial cover with room for light penetration. Often found in regenerating tropical forests, woodlands, or multi-layered systems. Suggests ecosystems are in transition --- gaining resilience and complexity over time.

-   75 to 95%: Dense canopy (resilient and functioning). Well-connected canopy structure. Indicates mature or maturing forests that provide critical habitat, regulate local climate, and show high resistance to disturbance.

-   95 to 100%: Closed canopy (thriving and saturated). Very dense, continuous forest cover --- typically seen in old-growth or wet tropical forests. These ecosystems are highly productive, carbon-rich, and functionally intact.

**Canopy moisture** measures how much water is present in vegetation based on how well leaves and vegetation reflect light. Healthy, hydrated plants reflect light differently than dry, stressed plants. The index we use, NDMI (Normalized Difference Moisture Index) ranges from -1 to 1, with high values generally indicating more water content in vegetation.

-   -1 to -0.8: Exposed soil.

-   -0.8 to -0.2: Stressed, dehydrated vegetation and/or sparse vegetation cover.

-   -0.2 to 0.4: Moderate water stress.

-   0.4 to 0.7: Moderately hydrated vegetation.

-   0.7 to 1.0: Lush, hydrated vegetation or waterlogging.

**Productivity** measures how green and photosynthetically active plants are. Healthy, photosynthesizing plants reflect light differently from stressed, sparse, or non-vegetated areas. We use three different indices for productivity -- NDVI, EVI, or MSAVI2 -- to understand productivity. We used NDVI for wet non-tropical islands, EVI for wet tropical islands because it better accounts for lush background vegetation, and MSAVI2 for arid and semi-arid islands because it better accounts for background soil reflectance. Productivity values range from 0 to 1, with high values indicating greater productivity.

-   0.0 to 0.2: Sparse, dead, or stressed vegetation. Plants are sparse or dead. May be common in unvegetated areas, degraded lands or during drought periods.

-   0.2 to 0.4: Moderate productivity. May be a common range of values for shrubland and grassland.

-   0.4 to 0.8: High productivity. May be a common range of values for dense vegetation and temperate forests.

-   0.8 to 1.0: Highest productivity. May be a common range of values for tropical rainforests with dense, productive vegetation.

Keep in mind: These measure of vegetation will not capture the full story of ecosystem change and recovery! These indices won't capture changes that unfold in the understory plants, nor can they tell us about species diversity or forest age. We recommend using all three indicators, along with your own knowledge of the island, to develop a complete picture of ecosystem recovery.
