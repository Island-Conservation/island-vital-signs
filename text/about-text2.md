**Canopy cover** measures the percentage of land covered by tree canopy and can help assess forest density and extent. The metric can also be used to assess the capacity of an ecosystem to support wildlife, regulate climate, and recover from disturbance. TCC is expressed as a percentage from 0% to 100%. It is a powerful way to track how ecosystems respond to invasive species, deforestation, restoration, or natural regrowth.

-   0 to 10%: Bare or deforested. Little to no tree cover. Can indicate heavily degraded landscapes, shrubland, grassland, or areas recently cleared by humans, herbivores, fire, or storms.

-   10 to 25%: Sparse canopy (early recovery or open ecosystems). Scattered tree cover or low-stature vegetation. May reflect recovering forests after disturbance, or naturally sparse systems like savannas. Alternatively, if a previously forested area becomes an area with sparse canopy cover, this may signal forest decline and an ecosystem that is vulnerable to erosion and invasive species.

-   25 to 75%: Moderate canopy (recovering or mixed forest). Partial cover with room for light penetration. Often found in regenerating tropical forests, woodlands, or multi-layered systems. May signal an ecosystem in transition that is gaining resilience and complexity over time.

-   75 to 95%: Dense canopy (resilient and functioning forest). Well-connected canopy structure. Indicates mature or maturing forests that provide critical habitat, regulate local climate, and show high resistance to disturbance.

-   95 to 100%: Closed canopy (thriving and saturated forest). Very dense, continuous forest cover, typically seen in old-growth or wet tropical forests. These ecosystems are highly productive, carbon-rich, and functionally intact.

**Canopy moisture** measures how much water is present in. NDMI (Normalized Difference Moisture Index) is a spectral index that measures differences in how vegetation reflects light. Healthy, hydrated plants reflect light differently than dry, stressed plants, allowing us to quantify vegetation moisture. NDMI ranges from -1 to 1, with high values indicating more water content in vegetation.

-   -1 to -0.2: Exposed soil or very sparsely vegetation cover.

-   -0.2 to 0.4: Moderate water stress.

-   0.4 to 0.7: Moderately hydrated vegetation.

-   0.4 to 0.7: Moderately hydrated vegetation.

-   0.7 to 1.0: Lush, hydrated vegetation or waterlogging.

**Productivity** measures vegetation greenness and photosynthetic activity. The three productivity metrics (NDVI, EVI, or MSAVI2) used in Island Vital Signs are spectral indices that measure differences in how vegetation reflects light. Healthy, photosynthesizing vegetation reflects light differently than stressed, sparse, or non-vegetated areas. We use NDVI for wet non-tropical islands, EVI for wet tropical islands because it better accounts for lush background vegetation, and MSAVI2 for arid and semi-arid islands because it better accounts for background soil reflectance. For all three metrics, values range from 0 to 1, with high values indicating greater productivity.

-   0.0 to 0.2: Sparse, dead, or stressed vegetation. May be common in unvegetated areas, degraded lands or during drought periods.

-   0.2 to 0.4: Moderate productivity. May be a common range of values for shrubland and grassland.

-   0.4 to 0.8: High productivity. May be a common range of values for dense vegetation and temperate forests.

-   0.8 to 1.0: Highest productivity. May be a common range of values for tropical rainforests with dense, productive vegetation.

We recommend using all multiple indicators, along with your own knowledge of the island, to develop a complete picture of the vegetation community on the island.
